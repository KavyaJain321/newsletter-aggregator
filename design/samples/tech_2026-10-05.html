The big one2-min read

OpenAI's whiplash week: a new agent out front, turmoil behind itReports differ

So what  OpenAI is shipping at full speed toward an IPO while its own staff, its lawyers and its politics pull the other way. How it handles that will shape how it is regulated.

At DevDay, OpenAI launched Dots, an AI agent with its own cloud computer and browser, plus a Decisions API and a new model, GPT-6.1 Sol. Dots goes straight at Grok Bot, and the obvious web address isn't OpenAI's: SpaceXAI owns dot.com, which redirects to the Grok Bot download page.

Behind the launch
LaunchedDots, the Decisions API and GPT-6.1 Sol, all announced at DevDay.
ReportedLegal exposure is mounting after OpenAI alerted more than 100 organizations to unauthorized activity linked to its models. Its president also pulled the second half of a $50 million pledge to an anti-regulation super PAC after staff pushback.
DisputedWhy people are leaving. OpenAI says three safety researchers mishandled sensitive information. Others point to unease over unregulated AI: another employee quit publicly on Saturday in The Atlantic, writing he had "decided that the current path is unacceptable."

OpenAI also reportedly hired former White House cyber official Thomas Lind to lead cyber and strategic risk policy. The three researchers and Saturday's resignation may be different people.

The split2-min read

Washington has a new AI task force. Who actually polices the labs?

So what  The White House is organising around AI but leaving risk to the companies. The FTC is moving the other way, with subpoena-like demands due within weeks.

On Sunday the White House announced a "Super Intelligence Force" led by the director of national intelligence and the FTC chairman, with the vice president, defense and Treasury secretaries and former AI czar David Sacks. It has 120 days to report on AI's risks and opportunities. It follows last week's safety pact between the White House and AI's top executives: called morally binding, but voluntary.

Side A

Let the companies handle it

Officials say the task force will defer to firms on managing risk.

Nvidia's Jensen Huang frames safety as an engineering problem, not a legal one, and he and Mark Zuckerberg argue the onus is on companies.

Side B

Use the laws on the books

The FTC's probe into OpenAI, Anthropic and METR is ramping up: an FTC official confirmed subpoena-like demands are expected within weeks.

Former FTC chair Lina Khan says regulators should rely on existing law, not on tech CEOs' manifestos and blog posts.

Two caveats: past FTC cases against big tech mostly ended in settlements, and November's midterms could hand Congress to Democrats, who may write AI rules of their own.

Hype check1-min read

"Robots can do 74% of physical tasks." Look closer.Self-reported

That headline made the rounds this weekend, about a new Anthropic report on robots and jobs. The report's actual numbers are smaller, and the most important one is tiny.

The fine print
In the reportExisting robots could handle work representing 34% of US working hours. Driving and warehouse jobs are most exposed; nursing and repair the least.
The catchRobots are cheaper than people for just 0.3% of tasks today, and that share may take 40 years to reach 10%.
UnexplainedThe 74% isn't explained anywhere in the coverage, and we couldn't trace it to the report.
Everything else that mattered 

Is the AI money real? Both camps got new numbersReports differ

Bain says AI's compute demand will require $6 trillion a year in revenue by 2031, about $4.2 trillion of it from products that don't exist yet. The five biggest spenders put $416 billion into capital investment in 2025 (all capex, not just AI); analysts expect $777 billion this year and $1.1 trillion in 2027. a16z argues cheaper AI expands usage. Amundi's chief investment officer says a correction isn't imminent, but will come.

Personal agents arrive, and they may come for your bank

Meta expanded Muse, including a small-business version and a Tamagotchi-like Muse Charm device; Manus shipped 2.0; OpenAI launched Dots. Apollo's Torsten Sløk warns of an "agentic bank run": if agents routinely sweep idle cash into higher-paying accounts, banks' funding costs rise.

Starship finally reaches orbit, then cuts the trip short

Flight 14 was Starship's first to reach orbit, deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites, but it landed after about 90 minutes instead of a planned 10 hours. NASA is counting on Starship to land astronauts on the Moon, where the US wants a nuclear reactor by 2030 and Russia and China are aiming for 2036.

Can AI be conscious? Three answers in one weekend

A Google DeepMind paper proposes a five-level framework for judging machine consciousness; by its measure, today's AI does well on the first level and poorly on the rest. Anthropic has been consulting theologians on the question, per The New York Times. Pope Leo XIV says algorithms lack "the spark of humanity."

When an AI explanation loses you, change the format, not the question

Andrej Karpathy's tricks for getting a better explanation: ask for a diagram first, ask for the answer "in HTML," or ask for it in ASD-STE100, the simplified English written for aerospace maintenance manuals. A looser version is to go "80% of the way" to that standard.
